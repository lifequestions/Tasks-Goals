Site architecture

One site, many lives

Your instinct on per-person pages is right. Your instinct on the banners is half right, and the half that's wrong would quietly cost you the thing that makes this spread. Here's the structure I'd build, and the argument for it.

Your proposal

What I'd keep, drop and add

Keep

A page per person

Obviously right, and it's the whole product. One page, one life, one link you can print on a card. Everything else is scaffolding around it.

Keep

“Start a page for someone” on every person's page

This is the growth loop, and putting it on the page itself is exactly correct — the people reading Luis's page are the ones who'll build the next fifteen pages. I'd only change where it sits.

Drop

A banner to the homepage

Think about who's actually on Luis's page: his sister, a grandson, an old colleague, arriving from a WhatsApp link. A bar across the top saying “Home” takes them out of a private family moment and into your marketing. It's the least valuable thing they could click, and it competes with his face for the first thing they see. Put the brand mark small in the footer and let the homepage be found by people looking for it.

Add

A page for a single answer

The easiest thing to leave out and one of the most important. One three-minute answer, with its own link and its own preview when pasted into WhatsApp. That's how a clip goes round a family on a Tuesday night, and how strangers first meet the product.

Add

Families, not just people

The bigger idea, argued properly further down: your own plan — Dad, then the in-laws — means related people. Treat the family as the container and you unlock something no competitor has.

The banner argument

Ask at the end, not the top

A promotional bar above someone's father is the product elbowing into a family's grief, and it converts badly for a plain reason: nobody wants to make a page for their own Dad until after they've watched somebody else's. The feeling that sells this — I should do this before it's too late — arrives about four minutes into an answer, not on arrival.

So the same invitation, moved: quiet mark at the top, the real ask at the bottom, and one contextual ask that helps the reader rather than selling to them — “ask Luis a question” — sitting right next to the questions he hasn't answered.

One exception worth making

If someone arrives on a page that has almost nothing recorded — a page seeded with a single answer — then a prominent ask near the top is fair, because there's nothing else to do yet. Show the invitation in proportion to how empty the page is.

Note what isn't here: no dashboard, no feed, no browse-all-pages, no public directory of the dead. A stranger should never be able to wander into someone's father.

URLs

These get printed on funeral cards. Choose carefully.

A person's link will be pasted into WhatsApp, written in a Christmas card, and stuck on a bench as a QR code. It has to be short, dignified, permanent, and impossible to guess your way into.

ShapeTrouble with it
/luis-james-de-souzaJust the nameBeautiful, and wrong. Two people share a name, someone squats the good ones, and anyone can test whether their father is on your site by typing his name in.
/p/7fK2mQ9xJust a tokenPrivate and permanent, but looks like a parcel tracking number on an order of service. It also tells the reader nothing when it arrives in a message.
/luis-de-souza-7fk2Name plus a short tokenReadable when pasted, unguessable in practice, no collisions, no namespace to police. This is what I'd ship.
/luisA vanity aliasLovely, so sell it. A custom short link is a natural paid extra and costs you nothing but a lookup table.

Two rules that go with it. A URL, once issued, works forever — if a name is corrected, the old link redirects rather than dies, because it may already be printed on two hundred cards. And every person's page carries a no-index instruction, so none of this turns up in a search for his name.

My counter-proposal

The family is the unit, not the person

Your own plan gives this away: Dad first, then your in‑laws. Those aren't unrelated strangers, they're two households — and in a few years your father's page will sit alongside his brothers and sisters, and eventually your mother's.

If every person is an island, you throw away the single most affecting thing this product could do: the same question, answered by four people who were there.

“Tell me about the house you grew up in”

Answered by your father. Then by his sister. Then by his brother. Three people, three versions of one kitchen, and the contradictions are the interesting part. No competitor does this, because they all treat a life story as a single-author book.

It's also the cheapest revenue you will ever find. The hard part of selling this is persuading someone that recording a parent is worth doing at all — and a family who has already done it for one person needs no persuading for the second. A page for Dad's sister is an upsell to a customer who is already convinced.

You don't need to build the family view now. You need the data to allow it: people belong to a family, answers belong to a question from a shared library, and the same question keeps its identity across people. Get that wrong in the first schema and the feature is a rewrite instead of a weekend.

The thing everyone forgets

A page has to survive the person dying

Today your father's page says 83 years old and still recording. One day someone will have to change that, and they'll be doing it in the worst week of their life. That should be one field and a gentle confirmation, never a support email.

What changes when it flips: a second date appears, the tone moves to the past, the highlights rise above the unanswered questions, and the unrecorded ones stop being a to-do list and become part of the record — questions he never got to. The page doesn't change. The state does.

States worth having from day one

  • Recording — alive, questions outstanding, progress shown
  • Complete — alive, finished, no to-do list nagging
  • Remembered — died, dates closed, highlights first
  • Sealed — a specific answer held until a date

Per-answer, not just per-page

  • Visible to anyone with the link (the default)
  • Family only — because he will name living people
  • Custodians only — kept, not shown
  • Never deleted by a relative in a row. Hidden, and recoverable.

Build order

What six pages need versus six hundred

  1. This monthNothing new. Your father's page is a file. Six families is six files in one folder — that is a working site, and the URLs can already be /luis-de-souza-7fk2.
  2. NextOne answer, one link. The share page for a single answer, with a proper preview card. This is the first thing that earns its keep.
  3. NextThe ask page. A form that adds a question to a page and emails the organiser. No accounts, and it's the loop.
  4. ThenThe organiser's console, so you stop being the person who pastes video IDs.
  5. ThenFamily grouping and the cross-family question view. Schema from the start, screen when you have two families who overlap.
  6. LastThe homepage. Genuinely last. Until a stranger can arrive without you, it's decoration.

Not building

Four tempting mistakes

  • A public directory of pages. It reads as a graveyard you can browse, and every family will hate it.
  • Accounts for viewers. His sister is 79. A link works; a password reset does not.
  • Comments under answers. Families argue. Let them record their own memory instead — same instinct, better outcome.
  • A follower count or view counter. Nothing about this should feel like performance.

Written for David, September 2026. Companion to the product and money plan.
