Product & money plan

Dad first, then everyone

A plan for turning the page we just built into something you can run for your father this autumn, hand to your in‑laws after that, and charge for by next summer. Written to be argued with.

What it is

One sentence, so every later decision has something to answer to

You film someone answering questions about their life, and we turn it into a page their family will still be watching in fifty years.

Everything below follows from three facts about that sentence. The person recording is usually old and not on the internet much. The person buying is their adult child. And the people who make it spread are the fifteen relatives who get sent the link — each of whom has a parent of their own.

That last group is the whole business. They are not customers yet; they are the next fifteen organisers. Design everything for them.

The plan

Five phases, and only the first one matters right now

Each phase has to produce something the next one needs. If a phase doesn't, skip it.

Now
4 weeks

Do it for your Dad, by hand

No product, no signup, no payment. You edit the HTML yourself. The only goal is to find out what actually happens when you point a phone at your father and ask him about his childhood.

  • Pick twelve questions, not sixty. Sixty will defeat you both.
  • Record in three sittings of about forty minutes. Ask from off camera.
  • Upload unlisted to YouTube, paste the IDs into the page, send the link to your family.
  • Write down every single thing that went wrong — where he froze, which questions fell flat, what your mother said when she saw it.
Output: one real page, and a list of what the product has to fix

Winter
8 weeks

Five more families, still by hand

Your in‑laws and three or four friends. You remain the machine: they film, you build. Painful on purpose — doing it manually is how you learn what to automate, and five families is enough to see the pattern.

  • Charge them nothing, but ask for two things: honest feedback, and permission to quote them.
  • Build the question pack properly — sixty questions in stages, with follow-up prompts. This becomes an asset you own.
  • Time yourself. If a page takes you four hours, that four hours is your spec.
Output: a question pack, six finished pages, six families who'll vouch for it

Spring
10 weeks

The self-serve version

The smallest thing a stranger can use without you. Resist building the clever parts.

  • Sign up, create a person, pick questions from the pack.
  • Upload video from a phone. Do not build in-browser recording — that's where products like this die. They film with the camera app they already know.
  • Auto-transcribe everything. It costs pennies and it unlocks search, captions and the book.
  • One private page per person, one payment, one "invite the family" button.
Output: someone you've never met pays you

Summer

The things people actually pay for

The page is the hook. The margin is in objects and permanence — this is where competitors make their money, and the part that survives the family arguing about whose parent it was.

  • The printed book, generated from the transcripts and photos.
  • The "forever" archive: a real promise, properly funded, plus a downloadable copy of every file.
  • QR cards for the funeral, a sticker for a bench or a headstone.
Output: average revenue per family roughly doubles

Year two

Stop selling to families one at a time

Every funeral director, celebrant, hospice and care home in the country is standing next to a family at exactly the moment this matters, and most of them are already selling an ugly memorial webpage. Be the thing they hand over instead.

  • Co-branded pages, they take a cut, you never pay for advertising again.
  • Care homes and hospices are the other end of it — recording while there's still time.
Output: a channel that grows without you

The money

Your sponsorship idea is right, with one change

The instinct behind it is correct: one person shouldn't carry the cost of something fifteen people will treasure, and £15 from each of fifteen relatives is painless where £150 from one is not. Group-funded emotional products work — it's why people happily chip in for a collective leaving card.

But never charge a relative to watch a recording that already exists

"Sponsor this page or you can't see Grandad" is the one move that could kill this. It turns grief into a turnstile, and the first person who says so on Facebook is right. Get this wrong once and the product is radioactive inside that family forever.

The rule instead: paying adds, it never unlocks. Everything recorded is free to watch for anyone with the link, always. Money buys more — more questions, the book, the printed cards, the guarantee it's still there in 2075. A relative chipping in isn't buying access, they're buying the family another twenty answers. Same revenue, opposite feeling.

Five ways to charge

Ranked by how likely they are to work

1. The adult child buys it as a gift

A one-off payment, bought by a son or daughter, given to a parent. This is the proven engine in this category — it spikes at Christmas, Father's Day and eightieth birthdays, and the buyer is emotionally committed before they see the price.

Core
Do first

2. The family chips in

Your idea, rebuilt as "help us finish Dad's story". The organiser starts free; any relative can put £10 or £25 toward the family plan, more questions, or copies of the book. Surplus goes to prints rather than your pocket — visibly.

Core
Growth engine

3. Objects: the book, the cards, the plaque

A hardback of the transcripts with the photographs, printed on demand. Twenty-five QR cards for the order of service. This is high margin, it's what people give at Christmas, and it's the only part of the product that survives the internet.

Core
Most of the margin

4. Funeral directors, celebrants, hospices

They hand it to the family; you give them a cut. Enormous reach, zero ad spend, and it catches the highest-intent moment there is. Slow to sell and needs the product to be finished first — but this is what turns a side project into a business.

Year two
Where scale is

5. A monthly subscription

Don't. Asking someone to pay £5 a month forever for their dead father's page means one day they have to decide to cancel him, and they will resent you for making that a decision. Sell permanence once, properly, and fund it.

Avoid
Guilt churn

The loop

How one page becomes fifteen

This is the only growth mechanism you need, and it's free. Every step has to be doable by a 68-year-old aunt on a phone.

01

One answer

The organiser records a single question about their parent and builds a free page. Ten minutes, no payment.

02

An invitation, not a video

What gets shared on WhatsApp and Facebook is a card: his face, his name, his dates, and "eleven questions still waiting". The recordings stay behind the link.

03

The family watches free

No signup, no wall. They see one answer and they see how much is missing.

04

They ask a question

The strongest move in the whole product. Free, takes ten seconds, appears on the page credited — "asked by Tom, aged 9" — and now Dad has a reason to keep recording.

05

They chip in

Some of them pay toward finishing it. All of them leave an email address.

06

They think about their own Dad

Every relative who watched has a parent of their own and has just been shown exactly what they'll wish they had. That's your next customer, acquired for nothing.

The metric that tells you it's working isn't signups — it's questions asked per page by people other than the organiser. If that number is above three, this spreads. If it's zero, nothing else will save it.

What it costs to run

The numbers that decide whether "forever" is honest

Ninety minutes of video per person is a realistic average. Where you keep it changes the answer completely, and it's the one technical decision with a moral edge to it — don't promise permanence you can't fund.

At roughly £1–2 a year, a £49 forever fee covers a very long time and you can say so with a straight face. At £4 a year it doesn't, and you'd be quietly relying on families forgetting. Build the cheap archive copy before you sell permanence.

What year one actually looks like: forty paid families at about £110 is £4,400, against maybe £600 of costs. That is not yet a business — it's proof, six good testimonials and a product that works. The business is phase five, or volume through the gift season. Decide now that year one's job is evidence, and you won't panic in March.

How to build it

Boring on purpose

Use

  • Phone camera for filming, straight upload. No recording in the browser.
  • One database, one page template, pages generated from data — exactly what we already have.
  • Automatic transcription of everything, from day one.
  • One-off payments and a "chip in" link. No billing system.
  • Private links with long random addresses, and a page that never appears in search.

Don't

  • An app. Nobody installs an app to interview their mother.
  • Accounts for relatives. An email address when they ask a question, nothing more.
  • Editing tools. If a video needs trimming, they'll re-film it.
  • AI that answers as the dead person. Say plainly that you won't do it — it's a real fear and refusing is a selling point.
  • Family trees. That's a different, crowded product.

Trust

The four things that will bite you

None of these are hypothetical. They are the standard failure modes of every memorial product, and handling them out loud is a genuine advantage.

Consent, recorded

Ask the person, on camera, whether they're happy for their family to have this. Ten seconds, kept as the first clip. It also makes a lovely opening.

Who owns it when they die

Name two custodians at the start. Families fall out, and the commonest support problem for pages like this is a sibling demanding control of a page another sibling paid for. Decide it while everyone's getting along.

Living people in the videos

Your father will name and discuss people who are still alive. You need a plain takedown route and the ability to make a single answer private without deleting it.

The promise if you stop

Write down what happens if you shut the company: every family gets every file. Publish it. It's the difference between a keepsake and a risk, and it costs you nothing to promise.

Names

Worth deciding before you tell anyone

What you call it changes who buys it. Warmer names sell to the child; plainer ones sell to the funeral director. I'd pick from the first three.
