Concept · a life told in recorded answers

Sixty questions.
Sixty windows.
One life behind them.

A wall of small closed windows, like an advent calendar. Each one holds a question about a person's life. They record the answer to camera, and the window opens onto their face, telling it in their own voice. Sealed windows stay shut until there's a video behind them — so the wall fills up slowly, and finishing it becomes something to do together.

How it works

Four steps, no film crew

The whole thing is one web page and a private YouTube channel. Nothing to install, nothing to pay for, and it keeps working after everyone involved has stopped touching it.

01

Pick the questions

Sixty is a good number — roughly one a week for a year. Family can submit their own, so a grandchild's question sits on the wall next to yours.

02

Record the answers

Phone on a stack of books, someone off camera asking. Two to six minutes each. The off-camera voice matters — people talk better to a person than to a lens.

03

Upload as unlisted

Unlisted YouTube videos don't appear in search or on the channel. Only someone with the link — or this page — can watch them.

04

Paste in the ID

Drop the eleven-character video ID into the question's entry. The window unseals itself and lights up. That's the entire publishing workflow.

The wall · ten example questions

Open a window

Ten questions grouped into reels, running roughly chronologically through a life. Four are lit — click one to see the player. Six are still sealed, which is what an unrecorded question looks like to a visitor.

The lit windows here play a stand-in clip so you can see the mechanism working. On the real site that frame holds your person.

Ways to shape it

Twelve ideas worth stealing

Release on a schedule

Windows can unseal on a date rather than the moment they're uploaded. One a week for a year, or all sixty at once on a birthday, or the last window only after they've gone.

A sealed final window

Record one message meant to be opened later — a specific date, a wedding, a fiftieth birthday. Everyone can see the window is there. Nobody can open it yet.

Let the family ask

A form that adds a question to the wall, credited: "asked by Tom, aged 9." Turns a solo project into something the whole family is building.

Print the question cards

One question per card, posted through the door a week apart. They think about it before the camera is on, which is where the good answers come from.

Audio-only windows

Some people freeze on camera. A photograph and a voice track over it is just as good — often better, because they forget they're being recorded.

Shuffle

One button that opens a random lit window. Rummaging through a shoebox of photos, not working through a syllabus.

Searchable transcripts

YouTube auto-captions can be pulled into the page. Then "Dad, what did he say about the boat?" is a search, not an hour of scrubbing.

A QR code on the order of service

The most quietly powerful use. People go home from the funeral and open the wall that night, and he's still talking.

Guestbook windows

A second wall where other people record their memories of them. Same mechanism, reversed: everyone else answering questions about one person.

Objects, not just questions

Some windows are a thing rather than a topic — the watch, the recipe, the scar. Hold it up to the camera and explain it.

Show the progress

"34 of 60 recorded" is a nudge while they're still here. Once it's a memorial, swap the counter for the dates.

Chapters as reels

Grouping by era gives the wall a shape and stops sixty windows feeling like a quiz. Roots, becoming, the middle years, what you learned.

Build notes

What this is made of

  • One HTML file. The questions live in a single array — adding a window is adding an object to it.
  • The iframe is only created when a window is opened, so sixty players never load at once.
  • Embeds use youtube-nocookie.com, which doesn't set tracking cookies until playback starts.
  • Unlisted videos are hidden from search and the channel page, but anyone with the link can watch — fine for family, not a password.
  • Free hosting on GitHub Pages, and the videos live on YouTube's storage rather than yours.
